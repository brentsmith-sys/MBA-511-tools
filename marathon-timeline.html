What happened

Marathon had a history of well over 100 years. Until about 2011 it was an integrated company: exploration and production upstream, refining and consumer fuel downstream, operating globally.

In 2011 it split in two. Marathon Oil, based in Houston, kept exploration and production. Marathon Petroleum took refining. Many executives, including the CEO, retired, and the company brought in Lee Tillman from ExxonMobil as its new CEO.

As the new team began defining the company's purpose and ambition, the price of oil fell to almost $29 a barrel through 2015. Offshore projects, which are expensive and take years to pay off, stopped making sense at that price. The team chose to focus on North America and on onshore resource plays.

The gap the change had to close

The culture they had

Large integrated companies tend to be process-oriented, slow, deliberate and step by step.

The culture the strategy needed

Resource play companies must be bold, fast, rapid and decisive, deploying capital and drilling quickly.

How they started rebuilding

Thirty-five employees from across the company spent a day in a hotel room naming the capabilities the new strategy required. They arrived at five:

Portfolio managementFinancial stewardshipOperational excellenceInnovationTalent

Days more went into defining the culture that would support those capabilities, then into aligning the organization around it. It was difficult, especially for people who were there because of the old culture.

What kind of change this was

RecreationMarathon's change was strategic, and it was a reaction to a shock rather than something the company saw coming. Professor Smith calls that combination a recreation, and the next video explains why recreations are hard to carry out.
